Pre-Anaesthetic Clinic Reference
Lyell McEwin Hospital · NALHN · South Australia — current perioperative guidance
This is a reference guide, not a protocol. It collates published national guidance and NALHN policy so you can find things quickly. It does not cover every patient, and it is not a substitute for clinical judgement or for local policy. Discuss any case you are uncertain about, or that falls outside what is described here, with a senior anaesthetist. Verify doses independently before prescribing.
Need consultant advice? Ask the consultant allocated to clinic, or the Duty Anaesthetist on 0466 153 358, or the remote anaesthetic consultant on call.
⚠️ Consider deferring elective surgery if: acute coronary syndrome or recent MI · decompensated heart failure · symptomatic severe aortic stenosis or severe mitral stenosis · significant uncontrolled arrhythmia · any coronary stent in the last 30 days — never stop antiplatelets without cardiology · systolic BP ≥180 or diastolic ≥120 · HbA1c >9% · SGLT2 inhibitor not withheld before major surgery (check ketones) · active asthma or COPD exacerbation, or chest infection. → Discuss with a senior anaesthetist.

Quick reference — common decision points
♥
Cardiac: when to consider delaying
  • Acute coronary syndrome or recent MI — cardiology first; the stent and antiplatelet window usually decides timing
  • Decompensated heart failure — NYHA IV, new or worsening
  • Significant arrhythmia — high-grade AV block, symptomatic VT, uncontrolled SVT, new rapid AF
  • Symptomatic severe aortic stenosis, or severe mitral stenosis
  • Stents: any stent in the last 30 days — do not interrupt antiplatelets. Drug-eluting stent for ACS: ideally 12 months or more; for chronic coronary disease: 6 months or more; time-sensitive surgery may be considered from 3 months
ACC/AHA 2024.

Blood pressure in clinic
  • Systolic under 180 and diastolic under 110: proceed — treat through the GP without delaying surgery
  • Diastolic 110–119 with systolic under 180: proceed with care — recheck after 5 minutes and screen for urgency
  • Systolic ≥180 or diastolic ≥120: consider deferring until a reading under 180/120 is documented
  • Take all antihypertensives on the day of surgery; ACE inhibitors and ARBs usually continue
  • Never stop beta-blockers or clonidine abruptly
AoA/BIHS 2026 · Heart Foundation 2016 · CALHN-GDE06093.

⬡
Diabetes key rules
  • Never omit insulin; basal insulin continues at the usual dose and time
  • Target peri-operative blood glucose 6–10 mmol/L
  • HbA1c above 9% (75 mmol/mol): consider deferring elective surgery
  • Metformin and gliptins: continue until fasting — morning list, hold the morning dose
  • Sulfonylureas: cease at least 24 hours before surgery
  • SGLT2 inhibitors: cease 3 days before if staying a day or more or having bowel prep; day-stay, the day of the procedure only. Ketones on the day
  • GLP-1 agonists: continue, with a clear-fluid diet for the 24 hours before
NALHN OWI01210 · OWI04030 · OWI05728; HbA1c band from ADS-ANZCA 2022.

✚
Anticoagulation snapshot
  • See the patient at least 7 days before surgery and give written instructions
  • Warfarin: stop 5 days before; check the INR 1–2 days before (needs to be under 1.5)
  • Apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran with CrCl above 50: last dose 24 hours before low-bleed surgery, 48–72 hours before high-bleed surgery. Longer with CrCl 30–50
  • No bridging for these three unless the interruption exceeds 96 hours in a high-risk patient
  • Bridge warfarin only at high thrombotic risk — for example a mechanical mitral valve, or VTE in the last 3 months
NALHN OWI02488.

Active conditions — consider delaying surgery
  • Acute coronary syndrome or recent MI — unstable/severe angina, recent infarct: refer to cardiology; revascularise and defer elective surgery until cleared (Class 1). No fixed interval in ACC/AHA 2024 — the stent / DAPT window usually decides timing.
  • Decompensated HF — NYHA IV, worsening/new HF
  • Significant arrhythmia — high-grade AV block, Mobitz II/III, symptomatic VT, uncontrolled SVT, new rapid AF, symptomatic bradycardia
  • Severe valvular disease — symptomatic severe AS; severe mitral stenosis (evaluate for valve intervention before elective surgery, Class 1)
What not to order — ACC/AHA 2024
  • No routine stress test or coronary angiography in patients with stable symptoms and adequate functional capacity, or before low-risk surgery — no benefit (Class 3)
  • No pre-op revascularisation for stable non–left-main CAD to reduce perioperative events (Class 3, no benefit — CARP, ISCHEMIA); it adds a stent and a DAPT window without lowering risk
  • Exceptions: left main ≥50% — revascularise and defer (2a); acute coronary syndrome — revascularise and defer (Class 1)

Heart failure
  • Optimise medical therapy pre-op (ACEi/ARB/ARNI, beta-blocker, MRA, SGLT2i — note SGLT2i hold rules)
  • Echo if new/deteriorating symptoms; EF guides anaesthetic strategy
  • Continue ACEi/ARB peri-op for HFrEF (2a)
  • Different rule when the indication is hypertension: in selected patients with controlled BP having elevated-risk surgery, omitting the dose 24 h before may be beneficial to limit intraoperative hypotension (2b)
  • Continue beta-blockers — never withdraw suddenly
  • No active fluid overload before elective surgery
  • High risk: peri-op BNP, plan HDU/ICU

Valvular disease
Click a lesion to expand ▾
Aortic stenosis ▾
  • Order an echo before listing when moderate or severe AS is suspected (murmur, old report, known valve) and the surgery is elevated-risk (Class 1)
  • Severe AS = valve area <1.0 cm² · mean gradient ≥40 mmHg · Vmax ≥4.0 m/s
  • Severe symptomatic AS — delay; refer for TAVI/AVR
  • Asymptomatic moderate or severe AS with normal LV systolic function and an echo within the past year — reasonable to proceed with elective low-risk surgery (2a). This does not extend to elevated-risk surgery.
  • Goals: sinus rhythm, adequate preload, avoid tachycardia & vasodilation
  • Caution with neuraxial unless haemodynamics tightly managed
Mitral & aortic regurgitation ▾
  • Echo before elective surgery when moderate or severe regurgitation is suspected (Class 1)
  • Meets criteria for valve intervention → consider intervention before elective elevated-risk surgery (Class 1)
  • MR: asymptomatic, normal LV function and PA systolic pressure <50 mmHg → reasonable to proceed (2a)
  • AR: asymptomatic with LVEF >55% → reasonable to proceed (2a)
  • Goals: avoid bradycardia and high SVR; afterload reduction (practice point)
Mitral stenosis ▾
  • Severe MS → evaluate for mitral valve intervention before elective surgery (Class 1)
  • If intervention is not possible: invasive haemodynamic monitoring is reasonable (2a); rate control (beta-blocker, CCB, ivabradine, digoxin) may be considered to prolong diastolic filling (2b)
  • Flag for cardiology review and consultant-level anaesthetic planning
Mechanical prosthetic valves ▾
  • Warfarin interruption and bridging follow NALHN OWI02488: bridge for a mechanical mitral valve with a stroke risk factor, tilting-disc / caged-ball valves, >2 mechanical valves, or an aortic valve with other risk factors; a bileaflet aortic valve with no AF or other risk factors is not bridged — Anticoagulation walkthrough
  • Minimal-bleed procedures: warfarin can continue
  • DOACs are not used with mechanical valves
  • Haematology / cardiology co-management; plan ≥7 days before surgery
Cardiac implantable devices (CIED)
  • Management plan before surgery whenever electromagnetic interference is anticipated (monopolar diathermy) — record device type (pacemaker / ICD / loop recorder / leadless / subcutaneous ICD), manufacturer, model, indication and pacemaker-dependence; last check date (Class 1)
  • Pacemaker-dependent + surgery above the umbilicus with EMI → reprogram to asynchronous or magnet on the generator (Class 1)
  • ICD + EMI above the umbilicus → tachytherapies inhibited by reprogramming or magnet; if pacemaker-dependent with a transvenous ICD, reprogram (Class 1)
  • Any device reprogrammed must be restored before discharge (Class 1) — book the device clinic for the post-op check when you book the pre-op one
  • Local route: cardiology device clinic (CIED interrogation and reprogramming) — flag on the chart and to the theatre team (process)

Hypertensive urgency vs emergency — Heart Foundation §2.1
  • Urgency: >180/110 with symptoms (e.g. severe headache) or moderate target-organ damage, not immediately life-threatening → oral treatment, review within 24–72 h; surgery waits
  • Emergency: very high BP (often >220/140) with acute target-organ damage — heart failure / pulmonary oedema, MI, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury, neurological change, encephalopathy, stroke → ED now; admission, usually ICU, parenteral therapy
  • Accelerated / malignant: retinal haemorrhages and exudates ± papilloedema — same-day specialist assessment
Higher risk of peri-operative BP swings — AoA Table 2
Consistently >180/120 · untreated hypertension · target-organ damage · ≥4 antihypertensives · postural hypotension · IHD, stroke/TIA, PAD, diabetes, CKD 3–5. Flag on the chart — the day-of team should shorten NIBP intervals and have a low threshold for an arterial line.
Rules that apply to everyone
  • Take all antihypertensives on the day of surgery; ACE inhibitors and ARBs usually continue (CALHN-GDE06093; AoA 2026 rec. 7 and ACC/AHA 2024 2a say continue). Never stop beta-blockers or clonidine abruptly. STOP-or-NOT (JAMA 2024): holding ACEi/ARB gave the same outcomes, so a held dose is not a cancellation
  • Postural screen — sit-to-stand BP if symptomatic, aged >80 or diabetic; a drop of ≥20 systolic or ≥10 diastolic within 3 min → review medication (AoA rec. 6)
  • Measure properly — seated, 5 min rest, validated cuff, two readings; if elevated, request the GP's or home readings rather than acting on one clinic value
  • Day-of target for the anaesthetist: MAP >70 and/or SBP >100 in higher-risk patients (AoA); ACC/AHA floor MAP ≥60–65 / SBP ≥90

COPD — COPD-X Plan 2024 §O9.4
  • No lung-function threshold is mandated for non-thoracic surgery — estimate risk/benefit for the individual; COPD roughly triples 30-day mortality after intermediate/high-risk surgery (3.4% vs 1.2%; §O7.1)

  • Who: prednisolone ≥5 mg/day (or equivalent) for ≥4 weeks by any route — oral, inhaled, topical, intranasal, intra-articular — and anyone with adrenal insufficiency. "If in doubt about the need for glucocorticoids, they should be given."

In the anaesthetic clinic — four things
  • 1
    Score the Clinical Frailty Scale in anyone ≥65 having elevated-risk surgery, and in younger patients who look frail. Under a minute; of all the frailty tools it is the most feasible and the most strongly linked to death and non-home discharge
  • 2
    CFS ≥5 = frail. Consider referral to geriatrics / perioperative medicine, and for elective or time-sensitive surgery consider delaying to optimise cognition, nutrition and comorbidity first; prehabilitation where there is time
  • 3
    Assess delirium risk — routinely in older patients. Frailty itself is the strongest predictor; add cognitive impairment, previous delirium, sensory impairment, polypharmacy, alcohol. Screen cognition (e.g. 4AT) (practice point) and flag on the chart so the ward plans for it
  • 4
    Shared decision-making — expected functional and cognitive outcome, not just survival; the non-operative option is a legitimate part of the conversation. Document goals of care and any advance care directive

Sip Til Send
is the default at LMH & Modbury
Unless the patient meets an exclusion, they sip approved clear liquids up to 200 mL per hour, right up until the ward is told they are called to theatre. There is no 2-hour clear-fluid cut-off for these patients.
Approved clear liquids
  • Water · diluted cordial · black tea or coffee (no milk, no sugar)
  • Max 200 mL/hour (200 mL biocup)
  • No sweets, lollies, antacids or chewing gum
  • Not clear soup, thickened liquids or jelly — despite the "clear fluid diet" label
Food still stops at 6 hours
  • No food in the 6 h before induction — regular and modified-texture diets alike
  • AM / all-day / emergency lists: fast from food from 0200
  • Afternoon lists (start 1230/1300): fast from food from 0630
  • Enteral feeds: cease ≥6 h, nil by tube until end of procedure
Other intake
  • ERAS carbohydrate drinks: continue to 2 h pre-op (≤200 mL/hr)
  • Oral medicines: up to 1 h pre-op with ≤30 mL water in total (a liquid premed such as sodium citrate counts); held medicines per Hold / Continue
  • Intubated ICU patient having a non-airway/thoracic/abdominal procedure — enteral feeds may continue
Excluded from Sip Til Send
  • GLP-1 RA or GIP/GLP-1 users — separate pathway, see below
  • Emergency abdominal surgery · gastrectomy · pancreatic surgery incl. Whipple
  • Previous gastric bypass or multiple laparotomies
  • Post-operative ileus · gastroparesis
  • NGT in situ with >500 mL drainage/24 h
  • Any NBM order, thickened fluids, or fluid restriction for a non-fasting reason
  • Anaesthetist has documented "NOT FOR Sip Til Send" in Sunrise (order details below)
  • The patient simply doesn't want to drink
If excluded: get a specific clear-fluid plan from the anaesthetist or home team and document it in Sunrise. Not covered by OWI05728: children; endoscopy, colonoscopy, cardiology and radiology procedures.
How to document it in Sunrise: Order entry → search "Communication Order – Fasting Instructions - Anaesthetic Procedures" → Add, then set the Sip Til Send Status dropdown to FOR or NOT FOR Sip Til Send. The order lands under the Nutritional Services banner.

Set "Requested For Date" to the date of surgery. It defaults to today and the order expires at 23:59 on the date selected — left on the default, a decision you make in clinic is gone by midnight.
Not automatic exclusions. Obesity, reflux and opioid use do not by themselves exclude Sip Til Send. Assess aspiration risk clinically — BMI ≥40, gastroparesis, symptomatic reflux or large hiatus hernia, previous bariatric surgery, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, opioid dependence, pregnancy >20 weeks, raised intracranial pressure — and if concerned document "NOT FOR Sip Til Send" in Sunrise, dated for the day of surgery. The anaesthetist decides on the day whether that means a rapid-sequence or awake technique.

Group & screen and getting bloods done
Group & screen — don't let this be the reason a case is cancelled ANZSBT 2025 §2.2.6, §2.8 · RCHT MSBOS 2025
Why it matters
  • No valid sample on the day means delay, cancellation or uncrossmatched blood. An unexpected antibody can take days to work up — the clinic sample is what gives the lab that time
  • LMH runs group & screen with crossmatch on demand, so the sample has to be in the system before the day
Sample timing
  • Ask and document two things: pregnant in the last 3 months? transfused in the last 3 months?
  • Neither → the sample is valid for up to 3 months (90 days). Tell the nurses it is a pre-op sample so it is flagged to freeze and stored
  • Either, or unknown → valid 72 hours only. Collect within 3 days of surgery (admission or the day before), not at clinic
  • A group & screen already on the system may not have been a stored one — if it was not flagged to freeze, repeat it
  • Send the patient to SA Pathology near the hospital so the sample reaches the LMH laboratory (practice point)
  • Known red cell antibodies → notify SA Pathology at LMH early so compatible units can be found (practice point)

ECG — ACC/AHA 2024 · NICE NG45
  • Reasonable before elevated-risk surgery with known coronary disease, significant arrhythmia, peripheral arterial or cerebrovascular disease, structural heart disease, or cardiac symptoms
  • May be considered in asymptomatic patients before elevated-risk surgery; the grid adds age over 65 with no ECG in 12 months (major surgery) and cardiovascular, renal or diabetic comorbidity (intermediate surgery)
  • Not recommended for asymptomatic patients having low-risk procedures
  • Antipsychotics, tricyclics and other QT-prolonging drugs — check the QTc (practice point)
Biomarkers (BNP / NT-proBNP, troponin) — ACC/AHA 2024 · ESAIC 2025
  • BNP or NT-proBNP is reasonable before elevated-risk surgery in known cardiovascular disease, age 65 or over, or age 45 or over with symptoms suggestive of cardiovascular disease. Pre-operative troponin in the same group is a weaker "may be reasonable"
  • Elevated: BNP >92 ng/L or NT-proBNP ≥300 ng/L → see Cardiac (cardiology input, post-operative troponin surveillance)
  • ESAIC: in high-risk patients (RCRI above 2) pair the natriuretic peptide with the DASI to judge cardiac reserve
Echocardiogram — NICE NG45 · SWAPNet 2021 · ACC/AHA 2024
  • Not routine. Consider for a murmur with any cardiac symptom, signs or symptoms of heart failure, or suspected moderate-or-worse valve disease or pulmonary hypertension — do an ECG first
  • Known heart failure with worsening breathlessness or a change in clinical status — reassess ventricular function before surgery
  • Ordering route and who can request — see Local pathways
Prompts for the "consider" cells (practice point)
  • Full blood count: expected significant blood loss, known or suspected anaemia (see Anaemia), haematological disorder, immunosuppression, liver or kidney disease, chronic inflammatory disease
  • Kidney function: age over 65, hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, diuretics, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, NSAIDs, SGLT2 inhibitors, bowel preparation, planned contrast, DOAC or LMWH dosing. Known chronic kidney disease: eGFR and proteinuria (ESAIC 2025)
  • Coagulation: known or suspected bleeding disorder, liver disease, haematological malignancy, major bleeding-risk surgery; warfarin — INR the day before. Routine tests do not measure DOAC effect

Hip fracture — ACSQHC 2023 · NALHN OWI04248 ▾
  • Why: older, frail, often anticoagulated; every hour of delay costs. Standard: surgery within 36 hours of first presentation, orthogeriatric care, nerve block and multimodal analgesia from the emergency department, cognitive screen for delirium risk
  • Antithrombotics are the usual reason for delay. Warfarin and antiplatelets: OWI02488. DOACs follow OWI04248 — dabigatran: stop on presentation; coagulation studies + thrombin clotting time + group & hold; proceed when thrombin time ≤20 s; idarucizumab via orthogeriatrics and haematology if the wait would exceed 48 hours. Apixaban / rivaroxaban: drug level (anti-Xa) with time of last dose; <30 µg/L = minimal effect; normal routine coagulation tests do not exclude active drug. The delay decision is multidisciplinary, not the clinic's alone
  • Neuraxial: same drug-level and interval rules as the Anticoagulation page
  • After surgery: enoxaparin 40 mg from 6 h post-op if haemostasis is secure (morning list → 1800, afternoon → 2200); restart of the usual anticoagulant is a consultant decision
  • Frailty and delirium: Frailty & Elderly
Bariatric surgery and the bariatric patient — SSI05003 · SOBA 2015 ▾
  • Who counts (NALHN): two or more of weight ≥120 kg, seated hip width >50 cm, BMI >40. Weight is assessed as a priority at pre-admission so the right bed, hoist and theatre table are booked — LMH bariatric rooms 1D/2D beds 13 and 20, ceiling hoists; equipment safe working loads are listed in SSI05003
  • Why: airway and desaturation, sleep-disordered breathing, metabolic syndrome, VTE. Weight and BMI go on the operating list; experienced staff; ramped position; drugs dosed to lean body weight (SOBA)
  • Clinic: STOP-BANG and the OSA plan (Obesity / OSA); HbA1c and the diabetes plan; GLP-1 agonist timing and fasting (GLP-1 / SGLT2i); airway assessment (Airway); group & screen for sleeve, bypass and band; anaemia check; VTE plan weight-adjusted
  • Caution with long-acting opioids and sedatives; monitored bed if OSA is severe or untreated
Phaeochromocytoma / paraganglioma — Endocrine Society 2014 ▾
  • Why: catecholamine surges on induction and tumour handling, then collapse after resection. Never operated on unblocked
  • Alpha-blockade first (phenoxybenzamine, or prazosin / doxazosin) for 7–14 days; beta-blocker only after alpha-blockade is established, for reflex tachycardia. Targets: seated BP <130/80, standing systolic >90; heart rate 60–70 seated, 70–80 standing. High-sodium diet and fluids in the last days to re-expand volume
  • Endocrinology co-manages: biochemical confirmation, imaging, genetics where indicated. Confirm blockade is adequate at the final clinic visit, not on the day
  • Consultant list, arterial line and large-bore access planned; ICU booked; glucose watched after resection
Major colorectal — colectomy, anterior and abdominoperineal resection ▾
  • Why: long surgery, blood loss, fluid shifts, cancer-related VTE and anaemia; many patients are frail
  • Clinic: anaemia found and treated ≥4 weeks out (Anaemia); group & screen; frailty screen and prehabilitation referral (Frailty); nutrition; diabetes plan; bowel-preparation fluid and electrolyte risk with diuretics, ACE inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors
  • Enhanced recovery pathway; epidural or regional discussion for open surgery; extended VTE prophylaxis (28 days) for abdominal or pelvic cancer surgery (practice point)
  • Stoma planning with the stomal therapy nurse before the day
Liver resection — ACG 2025 · AGA 2019 ▾
  • Why: blood loss from a vascular organ, post-operative liver failure if the remnant is too small or the liver is already diseased, and a coagulation picture that routine tests misread
  • Clinic: synthetic function — bilirubin, albumin, INR, platelets — with Child-Pugh and MELD (calculator); any cirrhosis or portal hypertension → the Renal / Hepatic rules (Child-Pugh C or MELD >20: postpone all but life-saving surgery; VOCAL-Penn includes surgery type); future liver remnant is the surgical and radiology team's call — confirm it has been made
  • Group & screen; anaemia treated ≥4 weeks out (Anaemia); cell salvage discussed with the surgeon; INR is not corrected with vitamin K or FFP to "make it safe" — viscoelastic testing where available; platelets <50 corrected before major surgery
  • Plan for a low central venous pressure technique, arterial line and a monitored bed; VTE prophylaxis individualised against bleeding risk (practice point)
Nephrectomy and cystectomy ▾
  • Why: blood loss, loss of renal mass, long surgery (cystectomy with ileal conduit)
  • Clinic: baseline eGFR and proteinuria, predicted post-operative function; nephrology input in chronic kidney disease (Renal / Hepatic); document a solitary kidney; avoid nephrotoxins and plan contrast (RANZCR rules); group & screen; anaemia optimisation; epidural or regional discussion
  • Cystectomy: enhanced recovery, nutrition, stoma planning; VTE prophylaxis extended as for pelvic cancer surgery (practice point)
Revision arthroplasty and major joint surgery ▾
  • Why: longer surgery and more blood loss than a primary joint, older and frailer patients, cement and tourniquet events
  • Clinic: anaemia treated ≥4 weeks out; group & screen at clinic and a repeat sample on admission for revisions; frailty screen (Clinical Frailty Scale) and prehabilitation; cardiac risk by the usual route (RCRI, DASI); antithrombotic plan written at clinic
  • Tranexamic acid and cell salvage per the surgical team; monitored bed if frail, cardiac or OSA (practice point)
Thyroidectomy with a large or retrosternal goitre ▾
  • Why: tracheal deviation or compression, possible difficult or impossible face-mask ventilation and intubation, tracheomalacia after resection
  • Clinic: ask about positional breathlessness and stridor; review the CT neck and thorax for tracheal narrowing and retrosternal extension; confirm euthyroid before elective surgery; full airway assessment and a written plan (Airway) — awake technique if indicated; consultant list; group & screen

Day surgery — who is suitable (NALHN OWI02467)
Clinic checks that the patient and the procedure suit day surgery at the site the surgeon has booked.
  • Social — discuss before it stays a day case: no responsible carer for 24 hours (unless local anaesthetic only) · no telephone · lives more than an hour away with nowhere to stay in Adelaide · language barrier to calling for help
  • Medical — think again if unstable, ASA 3 or higher: unstable angina · symptomatic valve disease · heart failure · MI within 3 months of the operation · uncontrolled hypertension · significant arrhythmia · cardiomyopathy · coagulopathy · poorly controlled COPD · severe or untreated OSA · unstable diabetes · severe thyroid disease · liver or renal failure
  • Also: anticoagulants or antiplatelets with no plan made, and a history of severe post-operative nausea and vomiting
  • Unstable condition: refer to the right physician or to anaesthetics to optimise for day surgery
  • Not suitable for day surgery: consider a short-stay admission (OWI02632)
  • Doubt about Modbury: discuss with a consultant anaesthetist and the surgeon
  • Going home (SSI02600): a responsible adult stays for 24 hours, and the first night is within an hour of the hospital unless the surgeon or anaesthetist has approved otherwise

Anaemia work-up
Arranging IV iron
Pre-operative anaemia raises transfusion, morbidity and mortality. Iron deficiency is the commonest cause and takes about four weeks to correct — so the work has to start at listing, not in clinic.
Anaemia = Hb <130 g/L, either sex (CPOC 2025 consensus target before moderate or major blood-loss surgery).

What clinic orders — CPOC 2025
  • Ferritin, transferrin saturation and CRP together — ferritin alone misleads because it rises with inflammation
  • Consider B12 and folate — not on routine pre-op bloods, so add them if the anaemia is not obviously iron deficiency.
Further work-up — reticulocyte haemoglobin, coeliac serology, thyroid function, LFTs — belongs with the GP or haematology, not clinic.

Who needs iron studies — and when
Send FBC + iron studies at listing if
  • Expected blood loss >500 mL (or >10% of blood volume), or any major surgery
  • Intermediate surgery with cardiovascular or renal disease, or unexplained symptoms
  • Anaemia on any recent FBC — that is the trigger to investigate, not to wait
  • Known CKD, inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, previous bariatric surgery, heavy menstrual bleeding, or a likely transfusion
Pre-assessment is usually too late to be the first screen. Minor surgery or blood loss ≤500 mL: proceed while investigation and treatment continue — do not cancel for anaemia alone.
Who does the optimising
GP — patient on the waiting list
  • NALHN policy: involve the GP for screening, investigation and iron replacement while the patient waits
  • The GP has SA HealthPathways (Anaemia management) and can arrange community IV iron and gastroenterology referral
  • Write it explicitly: the deficiency, what you want done, and the surgery date
Clinic — surgery is close
  • Too late to hand back and wait — decide with the surgeon and patient whether to treat and proceed, or postpone; a senior clinician makes that call
  • Document the type and likely cause of anaemia and the peri-operative plan
  • Complex or high-risk → refer to perioperative medicine; pharmacist referral where it applies — how to refer is on Local pathways
  • Haematology if Hb <70, no response to iron, or the diagnosis is unclear; nephrology for CKD anaemia or erythropoietin (practice point)
  • Cell salvage (LMH only): offered by LMH Anaesthetics for selected high-loss procedures — discuss and consent in clinic (MR82A); an option for patients declining donor blood (SSI00917)
Treatment timing
≥4 weeks to surgery
  • Oral iron — one tablet daily, or one or two tablets on alternate days (as effective, better tolerated)
  • Empty stomach; avoid tea; vitamin C does not help
  • About 4 weeks to move the Hb, 3–6 months to refill stores. Recheck before surgery
<4 weeks to surgery
  • Postpone, or give IV iron — shared decision with the patient and surgeon
  • Hb rises over 2–4 weeks; an infusion days before surgery does little for the Hb but still refills stores for recovery
  • Non-anaemic iron deficiency with big expected blood loss: consider oral iron — there is no reserve to lose
IV iron at any interval if
  • Oral iron contraindicated, failed or not tolerated
  • Ongoing blood loss
  • Poor absorption — inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac, post-bariatric, CKD

Principle: patients on chronic opioids or opioid substitution therapy (OST — Suboxone, methadone, depot buprenorphine) have a higher baseline requirement and are at risk of withdrawal, under-treated pain and respiratory events. Identify them in the PAC, confirm the prescriber and current dose, and make an explicit multimodal plan.

Local rule — NALHN OWI01938 (04/2026)
  • The usual therapy continues through the admission, to avoid withdrawal
  • In clinic: record the Authorised Prescriber, the dosing pharmacy, the drug, dose and formulation, so the ward can confirm them on admission
  • Dose changes — including splitting a methadone dose for acute pain — only with the Authorised Prescriber's approval
  • Extra immediate-release opioid for post-operative pain, and benzodiazepines, also need the Authorised Prescriber's approval, documented in the notes
  • Analgesia is not withheld. Maximise non-opioids; these patients may need higher than usual opioid doses
  • Depot buprenorphine: contact the Authorised Prescriber and get addiction medicine (DASSA) oversight
  • Advice: Acute Pain Service, the Drug and Alcohol Consultation Liaison Service, or DACAS on (08) 7087 1742

Chronic pain patient — PAC actions
  • Quantify the baseline: calculate oMEDD → and document it with the prescriber's name. ≥60 mg/day for ≥7 days = opioid-tolerant
  • Continue usual opioids (incl. patches) up to and including the morning of surgery — abrupt cessation risks withdrawal and worse acute pain
  • Pre-op opioid reduction may improve outcomes for elective major surgery, but only as a planned, supported taper with the prescriber/pain clinic — not initiated in the PAC on the day
  • Note gabapentinoids, benzodiazepines & cannabinoids — additive sedation with opioids; flag for the day-of-surgery anaesthetist
  • Set expectations: acute-on-chronic pain will be managed, not abolished; agree a functional goal and a discharge weaning plan back to baseline
  • High oMEDD (>100), complex pain, opioid use disorder, or major surgery → flag it on the chart; the anaesthetist on the day refers to the acute pain service
  • oMEDD >40 → recommend take-home naloxone and education; consider it with any outpatient opioid script
Assessment checklist
  • Current opioid type, route (incl. patches) & total daily dose → oMEDD calculator
  • OST agent, dose, prescriber & dosing point
  • Aberrant use, non-prescribed/illicit use, alcohol & benzodiazepines
  • OSA / sedation risk (compounded by opioids — see STOP-BANG)
  • Realistic expectations: counsel that pain will be managed, not abolished
Plan the analgesia
  • Multimodal & opioid-sparing: paracetamol, NSAID (if safe), regional/neuraxial, ketamine, dexmedetomidine, local infiltration
  • Continue baseline opioid/OST; add short-acting full agonist for acute pain on top
  • Avoid partial-agonist/antagonist combinations that precipitate withdrawal
  • Acute pain service referral on the day for major surgery / high baseline requirement — flagged from clinic
  • Naloxone & sedation-scoring plan post-op; caution with OSA
  • On the ward, when-required opioids are at least 2 hours apart, and slow-release opioids are not used for acute pain (NALHN OWI02563)
  • Discharge plan: defined opioid quantity, GP/prescriber handover, no open-ended scripts

Cultural safety & equity
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients in the pre-anaesthetic clinic
Ahpra 2020ANZCA PS62 2024AIHW 2010RHDAustralia 2020PBS Closing the Gap 2026
Cultural safety is judged by the patient, not by the clinician. It is determined by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander individuals, families and communities, and it asks us to reflect on our own assumptions and on the power we hold in the consultation (Ahpra 2020).
Not yet reviewed by NALHN Aboriginal Health. Contact details for the Aboriginal Health team and the interpreter booking process are still to be added.
In the consult
  • Ask every patient, whatever their appearance or name: "Are you of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander origin?" Self-report is the only reliable way to know, and it should be recorded (AIHW 2010)
  • Offer the Aboriginal Liaison Officer or Aboriginal Health Practitioner early — they can help with consent, transport, accommodation and discharge planning (practice point)
  • Ask who else should be part of the conversation. Communication may need to extend beyond the patient to family and community (ANZCA PS62)
  • Do not generalise. Ask this patient what matters to them; avoid assumptions based on any cultural group (ANZCA PS62)
  • Use plain language, check understanding, and book a professional interpreter where one is needed (practice point)
  • Travelling from a regional or remote community: try to complete the work-up in one trip, and confirm contact details and transport before they leave (practice point)
Rheumatic heart disease — ask specifically
  • Rates of rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease are high in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, especially from rural or remote communities, and affect young adults (RHDAustralia 2020)
  • Ask about rheumatic fever, regular penicillin injections, a known valve problem or previous valve surgery; listen for a murmur. Suspected and not yet assessed → echo and cardiology before elevated-risk surgery — see Cardiac
  • Penicillin injections: benzathine benzylpenicillin every 28 days. Find out when the next dose is due and make sure the admission does not cause a missed dose
  • Endocarditis prophylaxis: recommended for all people with rheumatic heart disease having a high-risk dental or surgical procedure; amoxicillin is first line. Flag it for the list anaesthetist
  • Anticoagulation: with moderate or severe mitral stenosis, or a mechanical valve, warfarin is the only suitable oral anticoagulant — plan it on Anticoagulation
Source: 2020 Australian guideline for acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease (3rd edition).
More common, and at a younger age
Screen as you would for any patient, with a lower threshold (practice point)
  • Diabetes and kidney disease — check HbA1c and renal function: Diabetes · Renal / Hepatic
  • Anaemia — screen and treat early: Anaemia / IV iron
  • Smoking — offer support to stop; Quitline 13 7848 has Aboriginal counsellors
Medicines and cost
  • Closing the Gap PBS Co-payment: registered patients pay the concessional rate, or nothing if they already hold a concession card. Brand premiums are not covered
  • Ask whether the patient is registered. Any PBS prescriber can register an eligible patient, once, through the Services Australia HPOS portal
  • It applies when the prescription is dispensed by an approved supplier, which includes approved public hospital pharmacies
  • Written instructions: the patient medicine sheet from the Med timing calculator can be read through with the patient or an interpreter
