Pre-Anaesthetic Clinic Reference
Lyell McEwin Hospital · NALHN · South Australia — current perioperative guidance
This is a reference guide, not a protocol. It collates published national guidance and NALHN policy so you can find things quickly. It does not cover every patient, and it is not a substitute for clinical judgement or for local policy. Discuss any case you are uncertain about, or that falls outside what is described here, with a senior anaesthetist. Verify doses independently before prescribing.
⚠️ Consider deferring elective surgery today if: ACS / MI within 60 days · decompensated heart failure (NYHA IV) · severe symptomatic aortic stenosis · uncontrolled significant arrhythmia · BP ≥200/120 (exclude hypertensive emergency) · HbA1c >9% without optimisation · SGLT2i-related ketoacidosis (check ketones) · DAPT after recent stent without cardiology sign-off · active asthma/COPD exacerbation or chest infection. → Delay, treat, and refer.

Quick reference — common decision points
♥
Cardiac: when to delay
  • Decompensated heart failure (NYHA IV, acute pulmonary oedema)
  • Unstable / severe angina, recent ACS (within 60 days)
  • Symptomatic or uncontrolled significant arrhythmia
  • Severe symptomatic valvular disease (esp. AS with HF)
  • Recent PCI: BMS <30 days; DES <3 months (ideally 6–12 months)
⬆
Hypertension thresholds
  • Proceed if BP <180/110 — optimise but don't delay
  • 180–200 / 110–120: discuss, optimise, reassess
  • >200/120: delay elective surgery, urgent review
  • No evidence cancelling stage 1–2 HTN improves outcomes (ESAIC 2024)
  • ACEi/ARB: continue OR hold 24h — equivalent outcomes (STOP-or-NOT 2024)
⬡
Diabetes key rules
  • Target peri-op BGL 6–10 mmol/L
  • HbA1c >9% (75 mmol/mol): consider deferring elective
  • Never omit insulin; basal insulin continues at the usual dose & time
  • Metformin & gliptins: continue until fasting starts
  • Sulfonylureas: cease at least 24 h before surgery
  • SGLT2i: cease ≥72 h pre major surgery; ketones day of
  • GLP-1: continue (2025) + 24h clear fluid diet pre-op
✚
Anticoagulation snapshot
  • Warfarin: cease 5 days pre-op; INR <1.5 target
  • Apixaban/rivaroxaban: hold 1 day (low-bleed) / 2 days (high)
  • Dabigatran: hold 2–4 days by CrCl
  • No bridging for DOACs (PAUSE)
  • Bridge warfarin only if high-risk (mech MVR, recent VTE)
Functional capacity (METs)
Poor <4 METs — high risk
  • Cannot climb one flight of stairs
  • Cannot walk at 6 km/h on flat
  • Limited to basic ADLs
Moderate 4–10 METs
  • Climb a flight / walk uphill
  • Golf, doubles tennis, dancing
  • Walk 6 km/h on flat
Good ≥10 METs — low risk
  • Swimming, singles tennis, football
  • Jogging / running
  • Heavy manual labour
ACC/AHA 2024: assess functional capacity with the DASI questionnaire (preferred over clinical estimate). Open the DASI calculator → Consider BNP/NT-proBNP if elevated risk + poor functional capacity.

Active conditions — DELAY surgery
  • Acute coronary syndrome or recent MI — unstable/severe angina, recent infarct: refer to cardiology; revascularise and defer elective surgery until cleared (Class 1). No fixed interval in ACC/AHA 2024 — the stent / DAPT window usually decides timing.
  • Decompensated HF — NYHA IV, worsening/new HF
  • Significant arrhythmia — high-grade AV block, Mobitz II/III, symptomatic VT, uncontrolled SVT, new rapid AF, symptomatic bradycardia
  • Severe valvular disease — symptomatic severe AS; severe mitral stenosis (evaluate for valve intervention before elective surgery, Class 1)
What not to order — ACC/AHA 2024
  • No routine stress test or coronary angiography in patients with stable symptoms and adequate functional capacity, or before low-risk surgery — no benefit (Class 3)
  • No pre-op revascularisation for stable non–left-main CAD to reduce perioperative events (Class 3, no benefit — CARP, ISCHEMIA); it adds a stent and a DAPT window without lowering risk
  • Exceptions: left main ≥50% — revascularise and defer (2a); acute coronary syndrome — revascularise and defer (Class 1)

Heart failure
  • Optimise GDMT pre-op (ACEi/ARB/ARNI, beta-blocker, MRA, SGLT2i — note SGLT2i hold rules)
  • Echo if new/deteriorating symptoms; EF guides anaesthetic strategy
  • Continue ACEi/ARB peri-op for HFrEF (2a)
  • Different rule when the indication is hypertension: in selected patients with controlled BP having elevated-risk surgery, omitting the dose 24 h before may be beneficial to limit intraoperative hypotension (2b)
  • Continue beta-blockers — never withdraw suddenly
  • No active fluid overload before elective surgery
  • High risk: peri-op BNP, plan HDU/ICU

Valvular disease
Aortic stenosis ▾
  • Order an echo before listing when moderate or severe AS is suspected (murmur, old report, known valve) and the surgery is elevated-risk (Class 1)
  • Severe AS = valve area <1.0 cm² · mean gradient ≥40 mmHg · Vmax ≥4.0 m/s
  • Severe symptomatic AS — delay; refer for TAVI/AVR
  • Asymptomatic moderate or severe AS with normal LV systolic function and an echo within the past year — reasonable to proceed with elective low-risk surgery (2a). This does not extend to elevated-risk surgery.
  • Goals: sinus rhythm, adequate preload, avoid tachycardia & vasodilation
  • Caution with neuraxial unless haemodynamics tightly managed
Mitral & aortic regurgitation ▾
  • Echo before elective surgery when moderate or severe regurgitation is suspected (Class 1)
  • Meets criteria for valve intervention → consider intervention before elective elevated-risk surgery (Class 1)
  • MR: asymptomatic, normal LV function and PA systolic pressure <50 mmHg → reasonable to proceed (2a)
  • AR: asymptomatic with LVEF >55% → reasonable to proceed (2a)
  • Goals: avoid bradycardia and high SVR; afterload reduction (practice point)
Mitral stenosis ▾
  • Severe MS → evaluate for mitral valve intervention before elective surgery (Class 1)
  • If intervention is not possible: invasive haemodynamic monitoring is reasonable (2a); rate control (beta-blocker, CCB, ivabradine, digoxin) may be considered to prolong diastolic filling (2b)
  • Flag for cardiology review and consultant-level anaesthetic planning
Mechanical prosthetic valves ▾
  • Warfarin interruption and bridging follow NALHN OWI02488: bridge for a mechanical mitral valve with a stroke risk factor, tilting-disc / caged-ball valves, >2 mechanical valves, or an aortic valve with other risk factors; a bileaflet aortic valve with no AF or other risk factors is not bridged — Anticoagulation walkthrough
  • Minimal-bleed procedures: warfarin can continue
  • DOACs are not used with mechanical valves
  • Haematology / cardiology co-management; plan ≥7 days before surgery
Cardiac implantable devices (CIED)
  • Management plan before surgery whenever electromagnetic interference is anticipated (monopolar diathermy) — record device type (pacemaker / ICD / loop recorder / leadless / subcutaneous ICD), manufacturer, model, indication and pacemaker-dependence; last check date (Class 1)
  • Pacemaker-dependent + surgery above the umbilicus with EMI → reprogram to asynchronous or magnet on the generator (Class 1)
  • ICD + EMI above the umbilicus → tachytherapies inhibited by reprogramming or magnet; if pacemaker-dependent with a transvenous ICD, reprogram (Class 1)
  • Any device reprogrammed must be restored before discharge (Class 1) — book the device clinic for the post-op check when you book the pre-op one
  • Local route: cardiology device clinic (CIED interrogation and reprogramming) — flag on the chart and to the theatre team (process)

Hypertensive urgency vs emergency — Heart Foundation §2.1
  • Urgency: >180/110 with symptoms (e.g. severe headache) or moderate target-organ damage, not immediately life-threatening → oral treatment, review within 24–72 h; surgery waits
  • Emergency: very high BP (often >220/140) with acute target-organ damage — heart failure / pulmonary oedema, MI, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury, neurological change, encephalopathy, stroke → ED now; admission, usually ICU, parenteral therapy
  • Accelerated / malignant: retinal haemorrhages and exudates ± papilloedema — same-day specialist assessment
Higher risk of peri-operative BP swings — AoA Table 2
Consistently >180/120 · untreated hypertension · target-organ damage · ≥4 antihypertensives · postural hypotension · IHD, stroke/TIA, PAD, diabetes, CKD 3–5. Flag on the chart — the day-of team should shorten NIBP intervals and have a low threshold for an arterial line.
Rules that apply to everyone
  • Take all antihypertensives on the day of surgery, including ACE inhibitors and ARBs (AoA 2026 rec. 7; ACC/AHA 2024 2a). Never stop beta-blockers or clonidine abruptly. STOP-or-NOT (JAMA 2024): holding ACEi/ARB gave the same outcomes, so a held dose is not a cancellation
  • Postural screen — sit-to-stand BP if symptomatic, aged >80 or diabetic; a drop of ≥20 systolic or ≥10 diastolic within 3 min → review medication (AoA rec. 6)
  • Measure properly — seated, 5 min rest, validated cuff, two readings; if elevated, request the GP's or home readings rather than acting on one clinic value
  • Day-of target for the anaesthetist: MAP >70 and/or SBP >100 in higher-risk patients (AoA); ACC/AHA floor MAP ≥60–65 / SBP ≥90
Antihypertensives — hold / continue
All classes continue per CALHN-GDE06093 (ACEi/ARB "usually continue") — drug-by-drug cards and the paste-a-list checker on the Hold / Continue page.

  • Metformin & gliptins — continue until fasting starts (ADS-ANZCA 2022 withholds them on the day)
  • Sulfonylureas — cease at least 24 h before (ADS-ANZCA withholds on the day)
  • Basal insulin — usual dose at the usual time (ADS-ANZCA reduces to ~80%)
Monitoring & escalation

  • BGL <7.0: commence 5% dextrose 60–100 mL/hr

  • BGL >18 on admission or pre-op: IV insulin per standard protocol — basal insulin is still given

COPD — COPD-X Plan 2024 §O9.4
  • No lung-function threshold is mandated for non-thoracic surgery — estimate risk/benefit for the individual; COPD roughly triples 30-day mortality after intermediate/high-risk surgery (3.4% vs 1.2%)
  • Optimise before surgery: smoking cessation · inhaled bronchodilators (regular LABA/LAMA ± ICS per the patient's plan) · pulmonary rehabilitation where time allows
  • Infective exacerbation or active wheeze — defer elective surgery until back to baseline (practice point)
  • Spirometry and DLCO are required only for lung resection (predicted post-operative function); otherwise test only when the result changes the plan — Investigations
  • Home oxygen: document flow rate and hours; flag for HDU planning. Plan for regional anaesthesia, early mobilisation, controlled oxygen and minimal respiratory depressants post-op
Asthma
  • All inhalers continue, including the morning of surgery — ICS withdrawal risks adrenal insufficiency under surgical stress (CALHN-GDE06093)
  • Well-controlled asthma is not a contraindication; check technique and adherence, step up if poorly controlled (practice point)
  • Active wheeze, or mid-way through an oral steroid course for an exacerbation — defer elective surgery until at baseline; respiratory review if control is poor (practice point — no guideline sets a timing)
  • Bring inhalers on the day; salbutamol pre-induction is the anaesthetist's call

Steroid cover — AoA / RCP / Society for Endocrinology 2020
  • Who: prednisolone ≥5 mg/day (or equivalent) for ≥4 weeks by any route — oral, inhaled, topical, intranasal, intra-articular — and anyone with adrenal insufficiency. "If in doubt about the need for glucocorticoids, they should be given."
  • Any surgery under anaesthesia: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction, then 200 mg/24 h by infusion (or 50 mg IM 6-hourly) while nil by mouth. Alternative: dexamethasone 6–8 mg IV covers 24 h
  • After: resume oral steroid at double the usual dose for 48 h (up to a week if complicated), then usual dose. Bowel prep: continue the usual dose; IV equivalent if prolonged NBM
  • Take the usual morning dose on the day of surgery (endocrine-replacement steroids need not be taken with food). Alert the anaesthetist — see the corticosteroid cards on Hold / Continue

Airway assessment
ESAIC 2025 §R6 (airway evaluation) · DAS 2020 awake tracheal intubation guideline · Cochrane 2018 bedside tests (Roth) · ANZCA PG07 2024
ESAIC 2025DAS 2020 ATICochrane 2018
The job in clinic is to predict the difficult airway and get that prediction to the consultant doing the list. A predicted or previously difficult airway must be documented, the patient told and consented for the likely technique, an airway alert issued for future procedures (ESAIC R6.6, 1C), and the day-of anaesthetist informed before the day.
In the anaesthetic clinic — four things
  • 1
    Read the old charts first. Previous anaesthetic records (Sunrise, scanned notes, other hospitals), airway alert letters, Cormack–Lehane grade, device used, and anything the patient was told. A previous difficult or failed intubation is the strongest predictor there is — and predicts difficult extubation too
  • 2
    Examine — the ESAIC minimum set, not one test. No single bedside test is reliable; combine them (R6.2, 1A). Record the six below plus the face-mask and front-of-neck predictors
  • 3
    Decide: is laryngoscopy, mask ventilation, a supraglottic device, or front-of-neck access predicted difficult — and is more than one of them? Two or more predicted difficult is the awake-technique conversation (below)
  • 4
    If difficulty is predicted: write it on the chart in plain words ("predicted difficult laryngoscopy — MO 2.5 cm, ULBT 3, fixed flexion"), tell the consultant anaesthetist for the list, book onto a consultant list where possible, and discuss the plan with the patient (awake vs asleep, consent for both). Give an airway alert letter if there has been a previous difficulty

Difficult mask ventilation & front-of-neck access
  • Mask: BMI >30 / neck >40 cm · beard · edentulous · Mallampati III–IV · snoring or OSA · age >55 · neck radiotherapy · mandibular protrusion limited (ESAIC R6 minimum set)
  • Front-of-neck: palpate the cricothyroid membrane — non-palpable (obesity, previous neck surgery, radiotherapy, goitre, fixed flexion) is a predictor in its own right (ESAIC Table 5); mark it if uncertain
  • Physiology counts too: pre-existing hypoxaemia, poor reserve, pregnancy at term, aspiration risk — they shorten the safe apnoea time and change the plan (R6.4)
When to think awake — DAS 2020
"Awake tracheal intubation must be considered in the presence of predictors of difficult airway management." The pattern is predicted difficult laryngoscopy plus predicted difficult rescue (mask, supraglottic device or front of neck), or a fixed anatomical reason the airway cannot be secured asleep.
  • Previous failed or very difficult intubation, or a previous "can't intubate, can't oxygenate"
  • Mouth opening <2 cm, trismus, or fixed jaw (TMJ ankylosis, mandibular fixation, post-radiotherapy fibrosis)
  • Fixed or unstable cervical spine — ankylosing spondylitis with fixed flexion, rheumatoid atlanto-axial instability, halo, unstable fracture, severe kyphosis where neck extension is impossible
  • Upper airway obstruction or distortion — stridor, laryngeal or hypopharyngeal tumour, large goitre with tracheal deviation, previous neck radiotherapy, deep neck infection, angio-oedema
  • Predicted difficult intubation with a full stomach where an RSI would leave no rescue
  • Morbid obesity or OSA with other anatomical predictors — obesity alone is not an indication
Awake needs a cooperative patient with a topicalisable airway — heavy bleeding, allergy to local anaesthetic, or a patient who cannot cooperate pushes the plan toward an awake tracheostomy or a carefully planned asleep technique with the surgeon scrubbed. Bring the patient's expectations in early: sedation is an adjunct, not a substitute for topicalisation (DAS).
Document, alert, communicate
  • Chart: the six measurements, mask/FONA predictors, the prediction, the planned technique, and "consultant anaesthetist for list informed — Dr X, date"
  • Sunrise alert for a known difficult airway; hand the patient an airway alert letter for future procedures (ESAIC R6.6) and tell them what it means
  • Consent the patient for the likely technique, including awake intubation if that is the plan (R6.6)
  • Extubation is part of the plan: any difficult intubation, airway-conflict surgery (maxillofacial, ENT, thyroid, cervical spine) or prolonged prone/robotic surgery predicts a difficult extubation — flag it
Frailty & the older surgical patient
ACC/AHA 2024 §3.3 · ESAIC 2025 R9 · ANZSGM 2022 position statement (Aus/NZ) · Clinical Frailty Scale (Rockwood, Dalhousie)
ACC/AHA 2024ESAIC 2025ANZSGM 2022CFS
In the anaesthetic clinic — four things
  • 1
    Score the Clinical Frailty Scale in anyone ≥65 having elevated-risk surgery, and in younger patients who look frail (ACC/AHA 2a; ESAIC 1C). Two minutes; it outperforms the other tools and predicts death, disability, length of stay and non-home discharge
  • 2
    CFS ≥5 = frail. Consider referral to geriatrics / perioperative medicine, and for elective or time-sensitive surgery consider delaying to optimise cognition, nutrition and comorbidity first (ESAIC R9.2); prehabilitation where there is time. Frailty roughly triples 30-day mortality and doubles complications
  • 3
    Assess delirium risk — routinely in older patients (ANZSGM). Frailty itself is the strongest predictor; add cognitive impairment, previous delirium, sensory impairment, polypharmacy, alcohol. Screen cognition (e.g. 4AT) (practice point) and flag on the chart so the ward plans for it
  • 4
    Shared decision-making — expected functional and cognitive outcome, not just survival; the non-operative option is a legitimate part of the conversation (ANZSGM; ACC/AHA). Document goals of care and any advance care directive

Perioperative fasting

NALHN OWI05728 06/2026ANZCA PG07 2024
Sip Til Send
is the default at LMH & Modbury
Unless the patient meets an exclusion, they sip approved clear liquids up to 200 mL per hour, right up until the ward is told they are called to theatre. There is no 2-hour clear-fluid cut-off for these patients.
Approved clear liquids
  • Water · diluted cordial · black tea or coffee (no milk, no sugar)
  • Max 200 mL/hour (200 mL biocup)
  • No sweets, lollies, antacids or chewing gum
  • Not clear soup, thickened liquids or jelly — despite the "clear fluid diet" label. Nothing containing fat, protein or insoluble fibre
Food still stops at 6 hours
  • No food in the 6 h before induction — regular and modified-texture diets alike
  • AM / all-day / emergency lists: fast from food from 0200
  • Afternoon lists (start 1230/1300): fast from food from 0630
  • Enteral feeds: cease ≥6 h, nil by tube until end of procedure
Other intake
  • ERAS carbohydrate drinks: continue to 2 h pre-op (≤200 mL/hr)
  • Oral medicines: most may be given up to 1 h pre-op with ≤30 mL water
  • Intubated ICU patient having a non-airway/thoracic/abdominal procedure — enteral feeds may continue
The caveats that matter
Excluded from Sip Til Send
  • GLP-1 RA or GIP/GLP-1 users — separate pathway, see below
  • Emergency abdominal surgery · gastrectomy · pancreatic surgery incl. Whipple
  • Previous gastric bypass or multiple laparotomies
  • Post-operative ileus · gastroparesis
  • NGT in situ with >500 mL drainage/24 h
  • Any NBM order, thickened fluids, or fluid restriction for a non-fasting reason
  • Anaesthetist has documented "NOT FOR Sip Til Send" in Sunrise (order details below)
  • The patient simply doesn't want to drink
If excluded: seek specific advice from the anaesthetist or Home Team about clear fluid intake, and document the alternative fasting plan in the EMR. Liaise with the treating team if in doubt.
How to document it in Sunrise: Order entry → search "Communication Order – Fasting Instructions - Anaesthetic Procedures" → Add, then set the Sip Til Send Status dropdown to FOR or NOT FOR Sip Til Send. The order lands under the Nutritional Services banner.

Set "Requested For Date" to the date of surgery. It defaults to today and the order expires at 23:59 on the date selected — left on the default, a decision you make in clinic is gone by midnight.
Scope — who this guideline does not cover
  • Paediatric patients
  • Endoscopy · colonoscopy · cardiology · radiology procedures
  • ECT patients on GLP-1 RAs — these patients should not cease their GLP-1; they fast from food and fluids from midnight, with normal morning medicines and a sip of water
Not automatic exclusions. Obesity, GORD and opioid use do not by themselves exclude a patient from Sip Til Send. Assess aspiration risk clinically, and if you're concerned, document "NOT FOR Sip Til Send" in Sunrise — this can be decided at the PAC consultation rather than left to the day, provided the order is dated for the day of surgery.

Why it matters: audits repeatedly show patients fast from clear liquids far longer than needed. Patients should not go ≥24 h without nutrition or hydration without a documented reason in Sunrise.

May need RSI or awake intubation — document clearly in the anaesthetic plan. These are aspiration-risk factors, not automatic Sip Til Send exclusions — if concerned, document "NOT FOR Sip Til Send" in Sunrise:

Pre-operative anaemia & IV iron
NALHN OWI02980 (11/2025) & SSI00917 (11/2024) · CPOC 2025 · BSH 2024 · NBA PBM Module 2
CPOC 2025BSH 2024NBA PBMNALHN OWI02980SSI00917
Why it matters: pre-operative anaemia is independently associated with increased transfusion, morbidity and mortality. It is common, detectable and treatable in the PAC. Iron deficiency is the commonest cause — detect and treat it weeks before surgery, not on the day.
Key threshold change: international consensus (CPOC/BSH 2024–25) now uses Hb <130 g/L in BOTH sexes as the trigger to screen and optimise before moderate/major blood-loss surgery — replacing the older sex-specific WHO cut-offs (120 women / 130 men) and the ≥80 g/L transfusion trigger that was wrongly used as an "optimisation target".
Which patients need iron studies?
Send FBC + iron studies if…
  • Any patient listed for surgery with anticipated blood loss >500 mL (or >10% blood volume) — CPOC standard of care
  • Any patient meeting NICE NG45 pre-op testing criteria for FBC: all major surgery; intermediate surgery with cardiovascular or renal disease, or unexplained symptoms
  • Hb <130 g/L in either sex on any recent FBC — that is the trigger to investigate, not to wait and see
  • Known CKD, IBD, coeliac, bariatric surgery, heavy menstrual bleeding, or an anticipated transfusion
Screen at listing, not at PAC. By the time they reach clinic there is often no time left to act.
The panel to order
  • Ferritin · transferrin saturation (TSAT) · CRP — ferritin alone will mislead you
  • B12 · folate
  • Renal function (creatinine/eGFR)
  • Reticulocyte haemoglobin content (CHr) where available
  • Consider LFTs; coeliac serology if malabsorption is plausible
Minor surgery or blood loss ≤500 mL: proceed with surgery while investigation and treatment continue — do not cancel for this alone.
Who optimises — PAC or GP?
GP / primary care
  • Patient on the elective waiting list with time before surgery — local policy is to involve the GP for screening, investigation and iron replacement
  • GP has SA HealthPathways support (Anaemia management) and can arrange community IV iron
  • Best placed to arrange endoscopy referral where the cause needs investigating
  • Write explicitly: the deficiency, what you want done, and the date of surgery
PAC / perioperative team
  • Patient already at PAC with surgery approaching — too late to hand back and expect a result
  • Deciding whether to postpone versus proceed — a shared decision with the patient and surgeon
  • Documenting the type and likely cause of anaemia, and the peri- and post-operative plan
  • Complex or high-risk: High-Risk Preoperative Assessment Clinic (Perioperative Medicine) — internal referral form → OPD1; expedite via Head of Unit
Also refer
  • PAC pharmacist — ≥65 y on any prescribed medicine · ≥5 medicines · insulin · any blood thinner other than aspirin · dose administration aid
  • Haematology — Hb <70, no response to iron, or diagnosis unclear
  • Nephrology — CKD anaemia, or where an ESA is being considered
Treatment — everything pivots on four weeks
≥4 weeks to surgery
  • Oral iron — one tablet daily, or one/two tablets alternate days (as effective, better tolerated)
  • Empty stomach — absorption falls up to 75% with food. Vitamin C does not help. Avoid tea and phytates
  • ~4 weeks to move the Hb; 3–6 months to replenish stores
  • Recheck before surgery — assume nothing
<4 weeks to surgery
  • Consider postponing, or give IV iron
  • Hb rises over 2–4 weeks — an infusion two days out does little for the Hb, though it still replenishes stores for recovery
  • Use shared decision making to inform any postponement of major surgery
IV iron at any interval if…
  • Oral iron contraindicated, failed, or not tolerated
  • Ongoing blood loss
  • Impaired absorption — IBD, coeliac, post-bariatric, CKD
  • EPO is not recommended routinely; may help in functional ID unresponsive to iron — a haematology/nephrology decision
Non-anaemic iron deficiency still matters. Where significant intraoperative blood loss or post-operative anaemia is expected, CPOC recommends considering oral iron — the patient has no reserve to lose.

The three pillars of patient blood management
1 · Optimise red cell mass
  • Detect & treat anaemia early
  • Treat iron / B12 / folate deficiency
  • Manage CKD anaemia
2 · Minimise blood loss
  • Stop/adjust antithrombotics appropriately
  • Tranexamic acid where indicated
  • Meticulous haemostasis; cell salvage for high-loss surgery
3 · Optimise tolerance of anaemia
  • Restrictive transfusion (single-unit, reassess)
  • Transfuse to symptoms, not a number
  • Optimise cardiorespiratory reserve

Intraoperative cell salvage — LMH only
  • Offered by LMH Anaesthetics for selected procedures; discuss & consent pre-op (MR82A) — the anaesthetist orders "Autologous washed red blood cells (Cell Salvage)" in Sunrise
  • Option for patients declining allogeneic blood — closed circuit with extension line possible; verify consent pre-op
  • Contraindicated with contamination by betadine, hydrogen peroxide, water/distilled water, alcohol, non-parenteral antibiotics, fibrin glue, collagen-based haemostatics
  • Leukocyte depletion filter used where malignancy possible, orthopaedic metal particles, or contaminated field (note: reports of hypotension with LDF)
  • Reinfusion expires 4 h after processing; must complete in theatre/PARU before ward discharge. Not with pressure bags
  • Potential complications: electrolyte imbalance, air/fat/cement embolism; salvaged-blood syndrome (rare)

Cefazolin cross-reactivity
  • Cefazolin has a unique side chain not shared with penicillins or other cephalosporins — cross-reactivity is very low (<1%)
  • For most penicillin-allergy labels without a history of severe/anaphylactic reaction, cefazolin surgical prophylaxis is appropriate
  • True anaphylaxis to penicillin / documented SCAR: discuss alternative prophylaxis (e.g. clindamycin/vancomycin per local antimicrobial guidance)
  • Document the de-labelling outcome in the record and notify the GP so the label is removed system-wide
Chronic opioids, OST & substance use
Continue maintenance therapy peri-operatively · plan multimodal analgesia · document the prescriber

Principle: patients on chronic opioids or opioid substitution therapy (OST) have a higher baseline requirement and are at risk of withdrawal, under-treated pain and respiratory events. Identify them in the PAC, confirm the prescriber and current dose, and make an explicit multimodal plan.

Chronic pain patient — PAC actions
  • Quantify the baseline: calculate oMEDD → and document it with the prescriber's name. ≥60 mg/day for ≥7 days = opioid-tolerant
  • Continue usual opioids (incl. patches) up to and including the morning of surgery — abrupt cessation risks withdrawal and worse acute pain
  • Pre-op opioid reduction may improve outcomes for elective major surgery, but only as a planned, supported taper with the prescriber/pain clinic — not initiated in the PAC on the day
  • Note gabapentinoids, benzodiazepines & cannabinoids — additive sedation with opioids; flag for the day-of-surgery anaesthetist
  • Set expectations: acute-on-chronic pain will be managed, not abolished; agree a functional goal and a discharge weaning plan back to baseline
  • High oMEDD (>100), complex pain, opioid use disorder, or major surgery → refer APS / pain medicine before admission
  • oMEDD ≥40 → recommend take-home naloxone + education (FPM PS01(PM))
Sources: ANZCA PS41(G) Position statement on acute pain management 2023 · FPM PS01(PM) & Appendix 2 (2025) · Acute Pain Management: Scientific Evidence 5th ed (2020)
Assessment checklist
  • Current opioid type, route (incl. patches) & total daily dose → oMEDD calculator
  • OST agent, dose, prescriber & dosing point
  • Aberrant use, non-prescribed/illicit use, alcohol & benzodiazepines
  • OSA / sedation risk (compounded by opioids — see STOP-BANG)
  • Realistic expectations: counsel that pain will be managed, not abolished
Plan the analgesia
  • Multimodal & opioid-sparing: paracetamol, NSAID (if safe), regional/neuraxial, ketamine, dexmedetomidine, local infiltration
  • Continue baseline opioid/OST; add short-acting full agonist for acute pain on top
  • Avoid partial-agonist/antagonist combinations that precipitate withdrawal
  • Early acute pain service referral for major surgery / high baseline requirement
  • Naloxone & sedation-scoring plan post-op; caution with OSA
  • Discharge plan: defined opioid quantity, GP/prescriber handover, no open-ended scripts

Why this page: the LMH / NALHN catchment serves a significant Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander population. Culturally safe pre-anaesthetic assessment improves attendance, optimisation and outcomes, and reduces did-not-attend cancellations. Ask respectfully about Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander identity for every patient and act on it.
In the PAC consult
  • Offer referral to the Aboriginal Liaison Officer / Aboriginal Health Practitioner early — support with consent, transport, accommodation & discharge
  • Check the patient understands the plan; use plain language and a professional interpreter where needed
  • Ask about a support person and family/community involvement in decisions
  • Flag travel from regional/remote areas — coordinate same-trip work-up to avoid repeat visits
  • Address did-not-attend risk proactively: confirm contact details, reminders, transport
Clinical considerations with higher prevalence
  • Rheumatic heart disease / valve disease — ask specifically; examine for murmurs; echo & cardiology if suspected (links to Cardiac)

  • Anaemia — screen & optimise early (Anaemia / IV iron)
